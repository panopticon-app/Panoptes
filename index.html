NRSWA 1991 — Statutory Sections

📖 s.54 — Advance notice of works

›
💬 Plain English

Before a utility company digs up the road they must warn the council in advance. For most jobs that's at least 10 working days' notice. For big jobs it's 3 months. Only genuine emergencies are exempt. If they just start digging without telling anyone — that's this offence.

NRSWA 1991 s.54
Advance notice of certain works

(1) Before beginning to execute any street works to which this section applies, the undertaker shall give the street authority such advance notice as may be prescribed of his intention to execute the works.

(2) This section applies to street works which are not emergency works.

(3) The notice shall be given not less than the prescribed period before the works begin.

Minimum notice: 10 working days (standard), 3 months (major works). Emergency works exempt — s.57 applies instead.
⚠️ FPN: £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted (pay within 29 days)

📖 s.55 — Notice of actual start date

›
💬 Plain English

Even after giving advance notice the company must confirm the actual date they're starting. If they turn up earlier than they said — or move the job forward without telling the council — that's this offence. Think of it as the second notification: "we told you we were coming, now here's the exact date."

NRSWA 1991 s.55
Notice of starting date of works

(1) An undertaker who has given notice under section 54 shall give the street authority notice of the date on which the works are to begin, not less than the prescribed period before that date.

(2) If the works are not begun on the notified date, the undertaker shall give fresh notice under this section.

(3) An undertaker who begins works without giving notice as required by this section, or before the notified date, is guilty of an offence and liable to a fixed penalty notice.

Commonly triggered when a job is brought forward without updating the street authority. A fresh notice is required any time the start date changes.
⚠️ FPN: £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted (pay within 29 days)

📖 s.57 — Notice of emergency works

›
💬 Plain English

If there's a burst pipe or cable emergency the company can start digging straight away — that's fine. But they still have to phone the council as soon as they possibly can after starting and explain why it's an emergency. Forgetting to call, or doing it hours later with no good reason, is this offence.

NRSWA 1991 s.57
Notice of emergency works

(1) An undertaker who executes emergency works shall, as soon as reasonably practicable after commencing the works, give the street authority notice of— (a) the place and nature of the works, and (b) the reason for treating the works as emergency works.

(2) An undertaker who fails to comply with subsection (1) is guilty of an offence and liable to a fixed penalty notice.

In practice notice is expected within 2 hours of commencement. The notice must state the specific reason — a vague response is not sufficient.
⚠️ FPN: £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted (pay within 29 days)

📖 s.59 — Duty to reinstate

›
💬 Plain English

Once the work is done the company must put the road back to at least the same standard it was before — properly, using the right materials and methods. They can't just dump some tarmac in the hole and leave. If the patch is badly done, too thin, wrong mix, or they never get round to the permanent fix — that's this offence.

NRSWA 1991 s.59
General duty of undertaker to reinstate

(1) An undertaker who executes street works shall reinstate the street to at least its condition before the works were executed (or better condition), and shall do so in accordance with any directions given to him by the street authority and the prescribed specifications and standards.

(2) The undertaker shall complete reinstatement within the prescribed period, and the duty to maintain the reinstatement continues for the prescribed guarantee period.

(3) An undertaker who fails to comply with this section is guilty of an offence and liable to a fixed penalty notice.

Standards set by SROH. Permanent reinstatement: 6 months (bound) or 3 months (unbound). Guarantee period: 2–3 years.
⚠️ FPN: £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted. Multiple FPNs possible for multiple openings.

📖 s.60 — Safety at the worksite

›
💬 Plain English

While work is going on the site must be made safe for everyone — pedestrians, cyclists, and drivers. Proper barriers, signs, and lights around any holes or hazards, set up correctly to the national code. If you arrive and there are no cones, no signs, an open trench with nothing round it, or the traffic management is a shambles — that's this offence.

NRSWA 1991 s.60
Safety measures

(1) An undertaker executing street works shall— (a) secure that the works are properly guarded and lit in accordance with the prescribed requirements, and (b) comply with such requirements as may be prescribed as to the use of traffic signs in connection with the works.

(2) An undertaker who fails to comply with subsection (1) is guilty of an offence and liable to a fixed penalty notice.

Requirements set by Safety at Street Works Code of Practice and Chapter 8 of the Traffic Signs Manual. Applies for the entire duration of works.
⚠️ FPN: £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted. Multiple FPNs possible. See also NCT Condition C1 (£500).

📖 s.70 — Ignoring council directions

›
💬 Plain English

The council can tell a utility company how and when to do their work — for example "you can only work between 9pm and 6am on this high street." If the company ignores a valid instruction and carries on regardless that's this offence. The council must have properly issued the direction first — verbal requests don't count.

NRSWA 1991 s.70
Directions as to timing and placing of works

(1) The street authority may give directions to an undertaker as to the time at which, or the manner in which, street works are to be executed, if the authority consider it necessary to do so— (a) for the purpose of preventing or reducing damage to the street, (b) preventing or reducing inconvenience to the public, or (c) in the interests of safety.

(2) An undertaker who fails to comply with a direction given under this section is guilty of an offence and liable to a fixed penalty notice.

Direction must be validly issued and formally served before this FPN can be used. Retain a copy of the direction as evidence.
⚠️ FPN: £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted (pay within 29 days)

📖 s.74 — Failure to notify completion

›
💬 Plain English

When a job is finished the company must tell the council straight away. The council uses this notice to start the clock on reinstatement guarantees and to update their street works register. Forgetting to send the completion notice, or sending it weeks late, is this offence. Note: staying on site too long is a separate issue — that triggers financial charges, not this FPN.

NRSWA 1991 s.74
Charges where works unreasonably prolonged

(1) The Secretary of State may make regulations providing for undertakers to be liable to pay charges to the street authority where street works are not completed within the agreed period.

(2) An undertaker shall also give the street authority notice as soon as the works are completed.

(3) An undertaker who fails to give notice as required is guilty of an offence and liable to a fixed penalty notice.

The FPN offence is failure to give completion notice — not overrunning. Overrunning triggers separate financial charges under SI 2009/303.
⚠️ FPN (completion notice only): £2,500 full / £1,875 discounted. Overrunning charges are separate.

🌡️ Cold-Lay — PCSM & DSM
A2.4 — temporary & Cat 3/4 permanent only
›
💬 Plain English

Cold-lay is tarmac that comes in bags or bulk from a van — it doesn't need heating before use. You can spot it on site: it's often darker and more uniform than hot-lay, may look slightly oily or greasy when fresh, and doesn't have the same rough aggregate texture as hot-lay AC. There are two types: PCSM (Permanent Cold-lay Surfacing Material) and DSM (Deferred Set Mixture). Cold-lay has its place — but it's often misused as a permanent solution on roads where hot-lay is required.

💧 PMMA — Polymer Modified Mastic Asphalt
A2.5 — flowable material for small openings
›
💬 Plain English

PMMA is a flowable bituminous material — it gets poured into small excavations and narrow trenches where you can't compact properly with conventional equipment. It self-levels and self-compacts as it cools. You can spot it on site by its very smooth, almost shiny black finish with no visible aggregate — it looks like a smooth patch of black rubber. It's increasingly common for telecom and small cable works. The key thing to check is whether it's been used where it's permitted and whether the surface finish is flush and crack-free.

🪨 Surface Dressing
S6.4 — Cat 3/4 only, not as standard permanent reinstatement
›
💬 Plain English

Surface dressing is bitumen sprayed onto an existing surface with chippings rolled in — it's a thin treatment, not a structural layer. You'll recognise it by loose chippings on or near the surface and a rougher, more uneven texture than hot-lay asphalt. It's NOT a standard reinstatement technique — it can only be used in very specific circumstances (Cat 3/4, lower-traffic roads) and requires HA agreement. If you see what looks like surface dressing used as a reinstatement on a busy road, that's a significant spec concern.

✂️ Trimback & Edge Preparation
S6.8 / S3.1 — saw cutting requirements
›
💬 Plain English

Before reinstatement, the edges of the excavation must be cut back cleanly with a saw — not just broken off. The cut edge must be vertical and straight. If you can see a ragged, broken or tapered edge at the joint between the new and existing material, the edge preparation was not done correctly. This is one of the most common causes of interface cracking and joint failure.

🌡️ Cold-Lay — PCSM & DSM
A2.4 — where it's permitted and where it isn't
›
💬 Plain English

Cold-lay is tarmac that comes in bags or a lorry and is applied at ambient temperature — no hot plant needed. There are two types: PCSM (Permanent Cold-lay Surfacing Material) which is approved for use as a permanent surface on lower-category roads, and DSM (Deferred Set Mixture) which is an interim/temporary material only.

The key on-site question is: is this cold-lay material being used where it's actually permitted? Cold-lay on a Cat 0, 1 or 2 carriageway as a permanent surface is a spec breach. It looks different from hot-lay — typically darker, more uniform, and slightly duller in appearance without the aggregate texture of HRA or SMA.

🧪 PMMA — Polymer Modified Mastic Asphalt
A2.5 — flowable material for small openings
›
💬 Plain English

PMMA is a flowable material — it's poured or pumped in rather than compacted. On site it looks very smooth and uniform, typically dark grey-black with a glassy or slightly shiny surface and fine texture. No stone chips visible. It's designed for small openings and narrow trenches where you physically can't get compaction equipment in.

Key things to check: it must be flush with the surrounding surface — any proud or sunken PMMA is a fail. And it must only be used in the situations the spec permits — primarily small openings and narrow trenches on any road category.

🪨 Surface Dressing
A2 / S6.4 — only permitted on Cat 3 & 4
›
💬 Plain English

Surface dressing is a bitumen spray followed by loose chippings rolled into it — it's a surface treatment rather than a structural layer. On site it looks like a layer of loose or embedded stones on a bitumen base, often with areas where stones have been picked off by traffic. It's the cheapest surface treatment and is only permitted as a reinstatement on roads where surface dressing is the existing surface treatment — and only on the lowest traffic categories.

The most common enforcement issue: surface dressing used on a Cat 0, 1 or 2 road where asphalt is required. That is a clear materials spec breach.

🔨 Compaction Requirements
S10 — what equipment, how many passes
›
💬 Plain English

Proper compaction is what makes a reinstatement last. If the layers aren't compacted enough, they'll settle under traffic and the surface will sink. Common site failures: wrong equipment for the material, layers too thick to compact properly, or compaction done too quickly. If you see a gang using a hand rammer on a main road subbase where a vibrating plate should be used — that's a potential spec breach.

Surface Material Reference
🟫 SMA — Stone Mastic Asphalt
A2.2 — high-performance surface course
›
💬 What it looks like on site

SMA has a very distinctive appearance — very high stone content, closely packed aggregate that's almost touching, with a dark binder just visible between the stones. It doesn't have rolled-in chippings like HRA — the stones are in the mix itself. On a reinstatement, the patch should match this texture closely. A smooth, fine-textured surface on an SMA road is wrong material.

⬛ AC — Asphalt Concrete / Dense Macadam
A2.3 — 6mm & 10mm close-graded surface course
›
💬 What it looks like on site

Dense macadam / asphalt concrete is the most commonly used reinstatement material — the standard black tarmac most people think of. The surface is relatively smooth with visible fine aggregate, no individual large stones or chippings sitting on top. A 6mm mix has a finer, smoother texture; 10mm is slightly coarser. Look for an even, dense surface with no open voids, ravelling or loose material. A good AC surface is consistent in colour and texture across the whole patch.

🌡️ Cold-Lay — PCSM & DSM
A2.4 — where permitted and where not
›
💬 What it looks like on site

Cold-lay comes out of a bag or small van — no hot mix plant required. It's commonly used as a temporary fix. You can usually spot it because it looks different to the surrounding road — often darker, sometimes slightly shiny, with a very uniform fine texture and no visible aggregate variation. It's softer in warm weather. The key question on site: is this being used as a permanent surface where it's not permitted?

🧪 PMMA — Polymer Modified Mastic Asphalt
A2.5 — flowable material, small openings
›
💬 What it looks like on site

PMMA is poured or pumped — not compacted. On site it looks very smooth and uniform, often slightly glossier than conventional asphalt. It flows around obstacles like valve boxes without needing compaction equipment. Most often seen in small openings and narrow trenches. A correctly laid PMMA surface should be flush with the surrounding road and have no visible voids or pull-back at the edges.

🪨 Surface Dressing
S6.4 — limited permitted use only
›
💬 What it looks like on site

Surface dressing is a thin treatment — bituminous binder sprayed on the existing surface with chippings rolled in. It's not a structural material. On site it looks like loose stones initially, or a thin chipped surface once trafficked. The question is whether it's acceptable as a permanent surface course for this road type. On most roads it isn't — using it where hot-lay is required is a clear spec breach.
